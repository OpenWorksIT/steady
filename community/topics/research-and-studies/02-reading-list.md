# The reading list
<!-- steady: pinned, wiki -->

A running list of papers on the ideas Steady is built around: measuring progress in therapy, asking in the moment, and what phones and watches can and can't tell us. This post is a wiki, so anyone in the community can edit it with the edit button under the post. Add a paper with a link (a DOI where there is one) and a line on why it's worth reading, and discuss it in a reply. The papers behind each part of Steady itself are in The science; this list is for reading more widely.

## Measurement-based care and feedback

- **Lambert, Whipple and Kleinstäuber (2018).** [Collecting and delivering progress feedback: a meta-analysis of routine outcome monitoring](https://doi.org/10.1037/pst0000167). *Psychotherapy* 55(4):520–537. Twenty-four studies: therapy with progress feedback did better than usual care in about two-thirds of them, with the clearest gains for patients predicted to do poorly.
- **de Jong and colleagues (2021).** [Using progress feedback to improve outcomes and reduce drop-out, treatment duration, and deterioration: a multilevel meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2021.102002). *Clinical Psychology Review* 85:102002. Fifty-eight studies and nearly 22,000 patients: a small but reliable effect on symptoms, and less drop-out.
- **Delgadillo and colleagues (2018).** [Feedback-informed treatment versus usual psychological treatment for depression and anxiety: a multisite, open-label, cluster randomised controlled trial](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(18)30162-7). *The Lancet Psychiatry* 5(7):564–572. 2,233 patients in eight NHS services in England; patients who were not on track did better when their therapists were alerted, measured with the PHQ-9 and GAD-7. The journal club's first suggestion.
- **Barkham, de Jong, Delgadillo and Lutz (2023).** [Routine outcome monitoring (ROM) and feedback: research review and recommendations](https://doi.org/10.1080/10503307.2023.2181114). *Psychotherapy Research* 33(7):841–855. Open access. What the evidence supports, where feedback doesn't help, and what gets in the way of using it.
- **Lewis and colleagues (2019).** [Implementing measurement-based care in behavioral health: a review](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2018.3329). *JAMA Psychiatry* 76(3):324–335. Fewer than one in five practitioners measure routinely, despite the evidence: the barriers for patients and practitioners, and what helps it take hold.

## Ecological momentary assessment

- **Shiffman, Stone and Hufford (2008).** [Ecological momentary assessment](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.clinpsy.3.022806.091415). *Annual Review of Clinical Psychology* 4:1–32. The classic review of repeated, in-the-moment ratings in daily life, and why they avoid much of the bias of recall.
- **Kleiman and colleagues (2017).** [Examination of real-time fluctuations in suicidal ideation and its risk factors: results from two ecological momentary assessment studies](https://doi.org/10.1037/abn0000273). *Journal of Abnormal Psychology* 126(6):726–738. Suicidal ideation changed markedly within a day for almost every participant, and the usual risk factors did little to predict the next few hours. A sobering read beside any between-session flag.
- **Myin-Germeys and colleagues (2024).** [The experience sampling methodology as a digital clinical tool for more person-centered mental health care: an implementation research agenda](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291724001454). *Psychological Medicine* 54(11):2785–2793. Experience sampling as a tool in therapy, not only in research, and what stands in the way.

## Digital phenotyping and personal sensing

- **Insel (2017).** [Digital phenotyping: technology for a new science of behavior](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2017.11295). *JAMA* 318(13):1215–1216. A short viewpoint: what passive data from phones might offer, and what it must prove, and earn, first.
- **Mohr, Zhang and Schueller (2017).** [Personal sensing: understanding mental health using ubiquitous sensors and machine learning](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-clinpsy-032816-044949). *Annual Review of Clinical Psychology* 13:23–47. How everyday sensors are turned into behavioural markers, and the limits: small samples, variability, privacy.
- **Huckvale, Venkatesh and Christensen (2019).** [Toward clinical digital phenotyping: a timely opportunity to consider purpose, quality, and safety](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-019-0166-1). *npj Digital Medicine* 2:88. What it would take for sensor data to be safe and useful in clinical care.
- **Torous and colleagues (2021).** [The growing field of digital psychiatry: current evidence and the future of apps, social media, chatbots, and virtual reality](https://doi.org/10.1002/wps.20883). *World Psychiatry* 20(3):318–335. A broad review of the evidence for digital tools in mental health care.
